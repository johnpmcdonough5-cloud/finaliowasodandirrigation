Caring for New Sod Through Iowa's First Hard Freeze

A few fall steps keep your turf strong through the cold months and ready to green up fast in spring.

Once the first hard freeze arrives, care for new sod shifts from active establishment to simply protecting what's already rooted in.

Watering Stops Mattering the Way It Did Before

Before the freeze, consistent watering was the priority for root establishment. Once dormancy sets in, the grass isn't actively growing, and the watering schedule that mattered so much in September and October is no longer doing the same job. There's no need to keep watering on the same routine once the ground has frozen.

Mowing Is Done for the Season

Once grass goes dormant, mowing has no benefit and can stress a lawn that's already adjusting to cold. If you haven't already gotten in a final fall mow at a moderate height, dormancy is the signal that mowing season is over until spring growth resumes.

Keep Traffic Off the Lawn

New sod is more vulnerable to damage from foot traffic than an established lawn, and that vulnerability doesn't go away just because the grass is dormant. Repeated paths across the yard, parked equipment, or heavy use during winter can damage the crown of young plants even while they're not actively growing.

What Comes Next Is Waiting

Once the freeze has hit and dormancy has set in, there isn't much active care left to do. The work that mattered happened in the weeks before the freeze, when the roots were establishing. From here, the lawn's job is simply to survive winter and pick back up once the soil warms in spring.

Common questions

Should I keep watering new sod after the first freeze?

No. Once the ground freezes and the grass goes dormant, the frequent watering schedule from establishment is no longer necessary.

Do I need to mow dormant sod at all over winter?

No. Mowing dormant grass has no benefit and can add unnecessary stress. Mowing season ends once the lawn goes dormant.

Is new sod more fragile than established grass over winter?

Yes, to some degree. Its root system is younger, so keeping foot traffic and heavy use off it through winter matters more than it would for an established lawn.
